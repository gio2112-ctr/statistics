Toy ECC Encryption versus the Caesar Cipher

Encryption, statistical cryptanalysis and attacks on a restricted key space

Notes

This homework compares two encryption schemes that sit at opposite ends of the spectrum: the Caesar cipher (a 26-key symmetric substitution) and a toy elliptic-curve scheme on secp256k1 whose private key is deliberately limited to a small range, so that attacks can be run in a browser. The same message is encrypted with both (sections 1 and 2) and the schemes are compared (section 3). Then each scheme is attacked with the method that fits it: statistics of the language for the Caesar cipher (section 4) and a search of the key space for the toy ECC scheme (section 5). Section 6 discusses the differences and the limits of both attacks.

The toy scheme is an ECIES-like hybrid construction (an elliptic curve key agreement followed by a hash-based keystream). It is written for learning and is not safe to use.

1 · Toy elliptic-curve encryption on secp256k1

The curve is y² = x³ + 7 over the 256-bit prime field of secp256k1, with the standard base point G of prime order n ≈ 2²⁵⁶. As in the previous homework a public key is Q = dG, but here the private key d is restricted to 1 ≤ d < 2K.

  1. Key generation (receiver). Choose the private key d and publish Q = dG.
  2. Encryption (sender). Choose a fresh ephemeral secret r. Send C₁ = rG. Compute the shared point S = rQ (it equals rdG), derive a keystream KS = SHA-256(S.x ‖ 0) ‖ SHA-256(S.x ‖ 1) ‖ … and send C₂ = m ⊕ KS.
  3. Decryption (receiver). Compute S = dC₁ (it equals drG, the same point), derive the same keystream and recover m = C₂ ⊕ KS.

Roles of the keys: d is the long-term secret and never leaves the receiver; Q is public and lets anyone encrypt; r is a one-time secret of the sender that makes every encryption different; S is the shared secret that only the two ends can compute.

2 · The Caesar cipher

Each letter is moved s places forward in the alphabet (wrapping around after Z): c = (m + s) mod 26. Decryption moves it back: m = (c − s) mod 26. The key s is shared and secret. Case, spaces and punctuation are left unchanged.

4 · Statistical cryptanalysis of the Caesar cipher

The attack uses a corpus of text in a known language (English). From the corpus it learns the frequency of single letters, of pairs and of triples, and the most common words. Given only a ciphertext, it tries the 26 candidate keys and picks the one whose decryption looks most like English. Three scoring methods are compared: letter-frequency analysis (χ² against the English letter distribution), n-gram scoring (log-likelihood under a letter n-gram model) and exhaustive search with a word test (count the common words in each of the 26 decryptions).

The model is trained on the first 70% of the corpus. Each trial takes a window of L letters from the remaining 30% (text never seen in training), encrypts it with a random shift and tries to recover the shift. Guessing at random would succeed 1 time in 26 (3.8%).

5 · Attacking the toy ECC scheme

The attacker knows the public key Q and the base point G (both public) and knows that 1 ≤ d < 2K. No property of the plaintext is used. Two searches are implemented, and each candidate is checked directly against Q:

  • Exhaustive search. Compute G, 2G, 3G, … with one point addition per step until dG = Q. Cost about 2K/2 additions on average.
  • Baby-step giant-step. Let m = 2⌈K/2⌉. Store the baby steps jG for 0 ≤ j < m, then compute the giant steps Q − i·mG until one of them equals a stored point; then d = i·m + j. Cost about 2·2K/2 additions and a table of m points.

The attack uses the key K, d and r chosen in section 1. Once d is recovered the attacker decrypts the message exactly like the receiver. Exhaustive search is limited to K ≤ 20 in the browser.

How the cost grows with K

For each K the attack is repeated on 5 random private keys (seeded generator). The counts are group operations (point additions).

6 · Discussion

Two different attacks. The statistical attack on the Caesar cipher is a ciphertext-only attack that exploits a property of the plaintext: the cipher moves the letter counts but keeps their shape. It needs a model of the language, becomes reliable only when the message is long enough for the counts to be stable, and can fail on short messages, where several keys give plausible text. The attack on the toy ECC scheme is a key-search attack that exploits a property of the key space: it needs nothing about the message, always works if the key range is known, and its success is certain, because a candidate can be verified exactly by checking dG = Q. Its cost depends only on K, not on the length or the content of the message. For the ECC ciphertext no statistical test helps: the output bytes look uniform, as shown in section 3.

Why this does not scale to a proper configuration. With a private key chosen uniformly in [1, n) with n ≈ 2²⁵⁶, exhaustive search needs about 2²⁵⁵ additions, and baby-step giant-step about 2¹²⁸ operations and a table of 2¹²⁸ points, which is far beyond any possible memory. Pollard's rho method removes the table but still needs about √n ≈ 2¹²⁸ steps. At the speed measured in section 5 this is many orders of magnitude longer than the age of the universe. No generic method does much better on secp256k1; this is why the best known attacks on the elliptic curve discrete logarithm are exponential, in contrast with the subexponential methods against RSA.

Other attacks and where the weakness comes from.

  • Weaknesses of the restricted key space. Because d lies in a known interval, Pollard's kangaroo method finds it in about 2K/2 steps with very little memory. A key chosen from a small set or from a low-entropy source (a short password, a timestamp) can also be found by enumerating the likely values, even if the interval is larger.
  • Weaknesses of the design of the toy scheme. Even with a 256-bit d the construction has flaws. It has no authentication, so it is malleable: flipping a bit of C₂ flips the same bit of the decrypted message and the receiver cannot notice. Reusing r reuses the keystream, so C₂ ⊕ C₂' = m ⊕ m' leaks the plaintexts (the same flaw as a one-time pad used twice). The sender must also check that Q lies on the curve to avoid invalid-point attacks. Real schemes such as ECIES or hybrid public-key encryption add a message authentication code or an AEAD cipher, a proper key derivation function and point validation.
  • Weaknesses of the Caesar cipher. Besides the tiny key space, there is the structural leak of the letter distribution. Increasing the key space (a general substitution) does not remove the leak, which is why the frequency attack still applies to it and why modern ciphers are designed so that the ciphertext is indistinguishable from random.

Take-away. Security needs both a key space that is too large to search and an output that carries no statistical trace of the input. The Caesar cipher fails both requirements; the toy ECC scheme satisfies the second one but not the first; a properly configured scheme satisfies both.
