Welford's Recurrences and Sampling Statistics

Online mean and variance, and their behaviour over m hackers and n systems

Exercise 1 · Welford's recurrence formulas

Notation

For a stream x₁, x₂, … let, after k values,

Mk = (1/k) Σi≤k xi   (sample mean)
SSk = Σi≤k (xi − Mk)²   (sum of squares about the mean)
s²k = SSk / (k − 1)   (sample variance, k ≥ 2)

Step 1 · Knuth's recurrence for the mean

Since k·Mk = (k − 1)·Mk−1 + xk:

Mk = ((k − 1)·Mk−1 + xk) / k
    = Mk−1 + (xk − Mk−1) / k

Write δk = xk − Mk−1 (the “surprise” of the new value). Then Mk = Mk−1 + δk/k, and two facts that we need below:

(a)  Mk − Mk−1 = δk/k
(b)  xk − Mk = δk − δk/k = δk·(k − 1)/k

Step 2 · Same approach for the sum of squares

Expand the sum of squares: SSk = Σ xi² − k·Mk². Subtract the same expression for k − 1:

SSk − SSk−1 = xk² − k·Mk² + (k − 1)·Mk−1²

Use (k − 1)·Mk−1 = k·Mk − xk on the last term:

SSk − SSk−1 = xk² − k·Mk² + (k·Mk − xk)·Mk−1
  = xk·(xk − Mk−1) − k·Mk·(Mk − Mk−1)

By (a), k·(Mk − Mk−1) = δk = xk − Mk−1, so

SSk − SSk−1 = xk·δk − Mk·δk = (xk − Mk)·(xk − Mk−1)

Welford's recurrence for SS:

SSk = SSk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)·(xk − Mk)

An equivalent form uses (b): SSk = SSk−1 + δk²·(k − 1)/k. Since the increment is a square times a positive factor, SS never decreases: the update is free of the cancellation that affects the formula with Σx².

Step 3 · Recurrence for the variance

With SSk−1 = (k − 2)·s²k−1 and the second form of the SS recurrence, divide by k − 1:

s²k = SSk/(k − 1) = ((k − 2)/(k − 1))·s²k−1 + δk²/k   (k ≥ 2)

In practice one updates SS and divides at the end: s²k = SSk/(k − 1) (sample variance) or SSk/k (variance of the sample as a population). Starting values: M₀ = 0, SS₀ = 0.

Code: the core is 3 lines

// JavaScript
function welford(xs) {
  let k = 0, mean = 0, ss = 0;
  for (const x of xs) {
    k++;
    const d = x - mean;     // delta_k = x_k - M_(k-1)
    mean += d / k;          // M_k  = M_(k-1) + delta_k / k
    ss   += d * (x - mean); // SS_k = SS_(k-1) + (x_k - M_(k-1)) (x_k - M_k)
  }
  return { k, mean, variance: ss / (k - 1) };
}
# Python
def welford(xs):
    k, mean, ss = 0, 0.0, 0.0
    for x in xs:
        k += 1
        d = x - mean             # delta_k
        mean += d / k            # mean update
        ss += d * (x - mean)     # sum-of-squares update
    return mean, ss / (k - 1)

Exercise 2 · Sampling mean and variance over m hackers

Setting. Each of the m hackers attacks n systems and obtains, for every system, an integer score in {0, …, 10} drawn independently from a fixed distribution with mean μ and variance σ². Conclusions

Comparison with μ and σ². The average of the sampling means over the hackers is very close to μ at every n, and the average of the sampling variances (SS divided by n − 1) is very close to σ², also for small n. The differences are only simulation noise, of order σ/√(mn) for the mean. Dividing the sum of squares by n instead of n − 1 gives an average of σ²·(n − 1)/n: visibly too small for small n, with a relative bias of 1/n that disappears only slowly. So the estimators x̄ and s² = SS/(n − 1) are unbiased: E[x̄] = μ, E[s²] = σ².

Why this matters in inferential statistics. μ and σ² are unknown in practice: one has only one sample (one hacker, or one set of measurements). Unbiasedness says that the sample statistics are right on average, and the n − 1 is exactly the correction that makes this true for the variance (one degree of freedom is spent estimating the mean). The spread across hackers measures the uncertainty of a single estimate, and is the basis of standard errors, confidence intervals and tests, for instance x̄ ± z·s/√n. Welford's recurrences make this practical for data that arrive as a stream (logs, scores, response times), with constant memory and without the numerical failure of the textbook formula.

Effect of the number of systems n. The standard deviation of the sampling mean across hackers decreases like σ/√n (the log–log plot has slope −1/2), and that of the sampling variance decreases like √((μ₄ − σ⁴(n − 3)/(n − 1))/n), also of order 1/√n, with a constant that depends on the fourth central moment μ₄ of the distribution: the bimodal distribution, with heavy tails, has a much noisier variance estimate than the binomial. Both estimators are therefore consistent (the law of large numbers), and their distribution over the hackers becomes bell-shaped (central limit theorem), as the histograms show. The number of hackers m does not change the quality of one hacker's estimate; it only reduces the noise in our picture of that quality.
