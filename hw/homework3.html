Toy ECC Encryption versus the Caesar Cipher

Encryption, statistical cryptanalysis and attacks on a restricted key space

Notes

This homework compares two encryption schemes that sit at opposite ends of the spectrum: the Caesar cipher (a 26-key symmetric substitution) and a toy elliptic-curve scheme on secp256k1 whose private key is deliberately limited to a small range, so that attacks can be run in a browser. The same message is encrypted with both (sections 1 and 2) and the schemes are compared (section 3). Then each scheme is attacked with the method that fits it: statistics of the language for the Caesar cipher (section 4) and a search of the key space for the toy ECC scheme (section 5). Section 6 discusses the differences and the limits of both attacks.

The toy scheme is an ECIES-like hybrid construction (an elliptic curve key agreement followed by a hash-based keystream). It is written for learning and is not safe to use.

1 · Toy elliptic-curve encryption on secp256k1

The curve is y² = x³ + 7 over the 256-bit prime field of secp256k1, with the standard base point G of prime order n ≈ 2²⁵⁶. As in the previous homework a public key is Q = dG, but here the private key d is restricted to 1 ≤ d < 2K.

  1. Key generation (receiver). Choose the private key d and publish Q = dG.
  2. Encryption (sender). Choose a fresh ephemeral secret r. Send C₁ = rG. Compute the shared point S = rQ (it equals rdG), derive a keystream KS = SHA-256(S.x ‖ 0) ‖ SHA-256(S.x ‖ 1) ‖ … and send C₂ = m ⊕ KS.
  3. Decryption (receiver). Compute S = dC₁ (it equals drG, the same point), derive the same keystream and recover m = C₂ ⊕ KS.

Roles of the keys: d is the long-term secret and never leaves the receiver; Q is public and lets anyone encrypt; r is a one-time secret of the sender that makes every encryption different; S is the shared secret that only the two ends can compute.

2 · The Caesar cipher

Each letter is moved s places forward in the alphabet (wrapping around after Z): c = (m + s) mod 26. Decryption moves it back: m = (c − s) mod 26. The key s is shared and secret. Case, spaces and punctuation are left unchanged.

4 · Statistical cryptanalysis of the Caesar cipher

The attack uses a corpus of text in a known language (English). From the corpus it learns the frequency of single letters, of pairs and of triples, and the most common words. Given only a ciphertext, it tries the 26 candidate keys and picks the one whose decryption looks most like English. Three scoring methods are compared: letter-frequency analysis (χ² against the English letter distribution), n-gram scoring (log-likelihood under a letter n-gram model) and exhaustive search with a word test (count the common words in each of the 26 decryptions).

The model is trained on the first 70% of the corpus. Each trial takes a window of L letters from the remaining 30% (text never seen in training), encrypts it with a random shift and tries to recover the shift. Guessing at random would succeed 1 time in 26 (3.8%).
