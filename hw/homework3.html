Penetration Scores: LLN and CLT

Simulating n systems attacked by m attackers: total, average and normalized score

Notes

Model. Each attacker tries the n systems one after the other. The result on the i-th system is a score Xi, and the scores of one attacker are i.i.d. Two models are used:

  • Bernoulli: Xi ∈ {0, 1}, P(Xi = 1) = p (1 = the system is penetrated). Mean μ = p, variance σ² = p(1 − p).
  • Rademacher: Xi ∈ {−1, +1}, each with probability 1/2 (a symmetric gain/loss, i.e. a random walk). Mean μ = 0, variance σ² = 1.

Three quantities for one attacker, after n systems: Sn = X₁ + … + Xn (total score), Sn/n (average score) and Sn/√n (normalized total score). The m attackers are independent, so they give m independent samples of each quantity, and the histograms below are built from these samples.

What to expect. By the Law of Large Numbers Sn/n → μ in probability. Since Var(Sn) = nσ², Var(Sn/n) = σ²/n and Var(Sn/√n) = σ², only the last normalization keeps a spread that neither explodes nor vanishes. By the Central Limit Theorem (Sn − nμ)/(σ√n) → N(0, 1) in distribution; for Rademacher this is simply Sn/√n.

Simulation settings

Both models are simulated at every run with the same m, the same maximum n and the same seed. The statistics are taken at the checkpoints n = 10, 30, 100, 300, 1000, 3000, 10000 (those not larger than the maximum n). The selector “Model shown” only changes what the sections below display.

3 · Law of Large Numbers: Sn/n → μ in probability

Convergence in probability means P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) → 0 for every ε > 0. The table estimates this probability as the fraction of the m attackers whose average is farther than ε from μ, and compares it with Chebyshev's bound σ²/(nε²) (a proof of the weak law) and with the normal approximation 2(1 − Φ(ε√n/σ)) given by the CLT.

4 · Variance under the three normalizations

Sample variances over the m attackers, with the exact values in the dashed lines. On a log–log plot, slope +1 means growth like n, slope −1 means decay like 1/n, and slope 0 means a stable spread.

5 · Central Limit Theorem (Rademacher case)

Histograms of Sn/√n for the Rademacher model (always shown, whatever the model selected above), against the standard normal density. The distance to the normal law is measured by the Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistic D = supx |Fm(x) − Φ(x)|. For comparison the table also gives D for the Bernoulli model after standardization, (Sn − np)/√(np(1 − p)).

6 · Interpretation: n systems and m attackers

Effect of the number of systems n. For one attacker the total score grows linearly in the mean (nμ) but only like √n in the spread. Hence the relative uncertainty of the total shrinks like 1/√n: against a large infrastructure the share of compromised systems is very predictable (Sn/n ≈ p), while the absolute number still fluctuates by several units. In the Rademacher (zero-mean) case there is no drift: the walk has no preferred direction, Sn does not converge (its typical size is of order √n, and it keeps crossing zero), but the average does go to 0. The trajectories of section 1 show exactly this: the average curves are squeezed towards μ, while the raw totals fan out.

Effect of the number of attackers m. The attackers are independent copies of the same process, so m does not change the distribution of one attacker's score. It controls how well the simulation (or a real set of observed attackers) reveals that distribution: probabilities are estimated with an error of order 1/√m, which is why the histograms are smooth for large m and ragged for small m. If instead one looks at the total over all attackers and all systems, T = Σj Sn(j), it is a sum of mn i.i.d. terms with mean mnμ and standard deviation σ√(mn), so more attackers push the expected damage up proportionally, while the average score per attempt is known with precision σ/√(mn).

Limits of the model. The conclusions rely on independent and identically distributed scores. Real attacks violate this: systems share vulnerabilities and credentials (one success makes the next more likely), attackers learn and adapt, and the impact of a penetration is heavy-tailed rather than 0/1. With positive correlation the variance of the total grows faster than n, the average concentrates more slowly, and the normal approximation can fail. The LLN and CLT then have to be replaced by versions for dependent or heavy-tailed variables, or the dependence has to be modelled explicitly.
