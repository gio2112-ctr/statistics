Modes of Convergence, Conditional Probability and Bayes

How random sequences converge (LLN, CLT, the role of √n), and how Bayes' theorem updates a belief

Exercise 1 · Modes of convergence in probability theory

The four modes

Let X1, X2, … and X be random variables on the same probability space. “Xn tends to X” can mean four different things, and they do not say the same thing about the data.

ModeDefinitionWhat it controlsMain use
Almost sure (a.s.)
Xn → X a.s.		 P( lim Xn = X ) = 1
⇔ P( supm≥n |Xm − X| > ε ) → 0 for every ε > 0		 The whole trajectory: for almost every outcome, after some n the sequence stays within ε of X forever. Strong Law of Large Numbers; Glivenko–Cantelli
In probability
Xn →P X		 P( |Xn − X| > ε ) → 0 for every ε > 0 One fixed large n: a deviation larger than ε is unlikely. Says nothing about whether deviations keep happening at later times. Weak LLN; consistency of estimators
Mean square (L²)
Xn →L² X		 E[ (Xn − X)² ] → 0 The average squared error, which also controls the size of rare big deviations. MSE of estimators, E[(X̄ − μ)²] = σ²/n
In distribution
Xn →d X		 Fn(x) → F(x) at every continuity point x of F Only the probability laws get close. Xn need not be close to X as numbers, and may even live on a different probability space. Central Limit Theorem; asymptotic tests and confidence intervals

How they are related

Strongest to weakest: a.s. ⇒ in probability and L² ⇒ in probability, and in probability ⇒ in distribution. The a.s. and L² modes are not comparable. Partial converses: convergence in probability implies a.s. convergence along a subsequence; if X is a constant, convergence in distribution implies convergence in probability; a.s. convergence together with a bound |Xn| ≤ Y, where E[Y²] < ∞, implies L² convergence (dominated convergence).

Three counterexamples (exact values)

  • A. In probability and L², but not a.s. (“sliding bump”). Let ω be uniform on [0, 1]. For n = 2k + j (0 ≤ j < 2k) put Xn = 1 if ω ∈ [j/2k, (j + 1)/2k), else 0. Then P(Xn = 1) = 2−k → 0, so Xn → 0 in probability and in L². But for every ω there is, at every level k, exactly one n with Xn = 1: the bump visits ω infinitely often, so Xn(ω) does not converge for any ω.
  • B. A.s. (and in probability), but not L². Let the Xn (n ≥ 2) be independent with P(Xn = n) = 1/n² and Xn = 0 otherwise. Since Σ 1/n² < ∞, Borel–Cantelli gives Xn = 0 for all large n almost surely. But E[Xn²] = n²·(1/n²) = 1 for all n: the rare spikes carry all the squared error.
  • C. In distribution, but not in probability. Let Z ~ N(0, 1), X = Z and Xn = (−1)n·Z. Every Xn has exactly the N(0, 1) law, so Xn → X in distribution; but Xn − X equals 0 for even n and −2Z for odd n, which does not tend to 0.

Their role in the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem

Let X1, X2, … be independent and identically distributed with mean μ and variance σ² < ∞, and X̄n = (X1 + … + Xn)/n.

  • L² and the weak LLN. Independence makes the variances add, so E[(X̄n − μ)²] = Var(X̄n) = σ²/n → 0: the sample mean converges to μ in mean square. Chebyshev's inequality then turns it into convergence in probability, with an explicit rate: P(|X̄n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²). (Khinchin: for i.i.d. data a finite mean is enough, without variance.)
  • A.s. and the strong LLN. Kolmogorov: for i.i.d. data with finite mean, X̄n → μ almost surely. This is the stronger statement about the single sequence of data that one actually observes: from some (random) n on, the running average stays near μ. The weak law alone does not forbid occasional deviations at arbitrarily late times.
  • Distribution and the CLT. Zn = √n (X̄n − μ)/σ →d N(0, 1). The theorem is about laws only and cannot be upgraded: Zn does not converge in probability (not even to a random variable), because Zn and Z2n stay at distance of order 1. The CLT gives the shape and size of the error of X̄n, which the LLN only says goes to 0.
  • Without a mean there is no law of large numbers. For the Cauchy distribution X̄n has exactly the same law as X1 for every n, so it never concentrates (simulated below).

Other results of the same kind in statistics.

  • Consistency of an estimator θ̂n is convergence in probability to θ (strong consistency: a.s.); the MSE (L²) of θ̂n = variance + bias² controls it.
  • Slutsky's theorem combines the modes: if Zn →d Z and Sn →P c, then Zn/Sn →d Z/c. This justifies replacing the unknown σ by the sample standard deviation s in the CLT, and gives the t-statistic its N(0, 1) limit.
  • Delta method and asymptotic normality of the MLE: √n (θ̂n − θ) →d N(0, 1/I(θ)), the basis of Wald confidence intervals and tests.
  • Glivenko–Cantelli: the empirical distribution function converges to F uniformly, almost surely: the sample describes the population.
  • Monte Carlo integration: the error of an average of n samples is of order σ/√n, from the LLN (it converges) and the CLT (how fast).

Why √n appears

Everything comes from the additivity of variance. If X1, …, Xn are independent (or just uncorrelated) with variance σ², the covariance terms vanish:

Var(Sn) = Σi Var(Xi) + 2 Σi<j Cov(Xi, Xj) = nσ²  (covariances = 0)
sd(Sn) = σ√n  (the sum spreads like √n, not like n)
sd(X̄n) = sd(Sn)/n = σ/√n  (the mean concentrates like 1/√n)
sd( √n (X̄n − μ) ) = σ  (√n is the only scaling that leaves a non-degenerate limit)

With the factor n the limit is 0 (the LLN); with a smaller power of n it would blow up; with √n the spread stays at σ, which is why the CLT is stated for √n (X̄n − μ). In statistical inference this is the standard error σ/√n: a 95% confidence interval is x̄ ± 1.96 s/√n, the t statistic is √n (x̄ − μ0)/s, and the precision of any average improves only with the square root of the sample size (four times more data to halve the error). For example, to halve the uncertainty on the mean response time of a service one must measure four times as many requests.

Independence is essential for this scaling. If the Xi have correlation ρ ≥ 0 the covariances add n(n − 1)ρσ² to the variance; with ρ > 0 fixed, Var(X̄n) → ρσ² and the mean no longer converges to μ. For weakly dependent series (decaying correlations) the √n scaling survives, but the constant changes: for an AR(1) series with parameter ρ, Var(Sn) ≈ nσ²(1 + ρ)/(1 − ρ), and the effective sample size is n(1 − ρ)/(1 + ρ). Log events and network measurements are often autocorrelated, so the nominal n overstates the information they contain.

Conclusions of Exercise 1

The four modes measure different things: a.s. the whole trajectory, in probability a single time, L² the mean squared error, in distribution only the law. The LLN lives in the strong modes (L² and in probability for the weak law, a.s. for the strong law) and says that X̄n reaches μ; the CLT lives in the weakest mode and says how it approaches μ, with error σ/√n and a Gaussian shape. The factor √n is the signature of independence through the additivity of variance: lose independence and the rate and even the limit change; lose the mean (Cauchy) and there is no law of large numbers at all.

Exercise 2 · Conditional probability, Bayes' theorem and statistical inference

Conditional probability

In probability theory, the conditional probability of A given B is the probability of A when we restrict attention to the outcomes in B:

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B),  P(B) > 0
multiplication rule: P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) P(B) = P(B | A) P(A)
total probability: P(B) = Σi P(B | Ai) P(Ai)  for a partition A1, …, Ak
independence: P(A | B) = P(A)  ⇔  P(A ∩ B) = P(A) P(B)

In statistics the same quantity is a conditional relative frequency of a contingency table, f(F | X) = nij/ni· (as in Homework 6), and independence is exactly the statement that all conditional distributions are equal. Conditioning is also how we describe a model: a regression is a conditional mean E[Y | X], and a likelihood is a conditional probability of the data given the parameters.

Bayes' theorem and the three components

Swapping the roles of A and B in the multiplication rule gives Bayes' theorem. For a hypothesis (or a parameter value) H and observed data D:

P(H | D) = P(D | H) · P(H) / P(D),  P(D) = Σh P(D | h) P(h)
for a continuous parameter θ:  π(θ | x) = L(θ; x) π(θ) / ∫ L(t; x) π(t) dt  ∝  L(θ; x) · π(θ)
posterior ∝ likelihood × prior   (the denominator only normalises)
ComponentMeaningWhere it comes from
Prior P(H), π(θ)What we believe about H before seeing the data.Earlier studies, base rates, expert knowledge, or a deliberately vague choice.
Likelihood P(D | H), L(θ; x)How probable the observed data are if H (or θ) were true. As a function of θ it is not a probability distribution over θ.The statistical model of the data-generating process.
Posterior P(H | D), π(θ | x)What we believe about H after seeing the data: the answer.Prior × likelihood, normalised by the evidence P(D).

Why updating is central. Classical inference gives P(D | H), the probability of the data under a hypothesis. What we usually want is P(H | D), how probable the hypothesis is given the data, and Bayes' theorem is the only coherent way to get it: it converts “how well H explains the data” into “how much H is believed”, with the prior as the starting point. The posterior of one batch of data becomes the prior for the next, so evidence accumulates; for independent data the final posterior does not depend on the order or the grouping of the batches. In odds form the update is simply posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio (the Bayes factor).

Example B · Prior and likelihood on a parameter: the Beta–Binomial model

Let θ be the unknown fraction of login attempts that are malicious. We observe n attempts, k of them malicious: likelihood L(θ) ∝ θk(1 − θ)n−k (Binomial). With a Beta(a, b) prior, π(θ) ∝ θa−1(1 − θ)b−1, the posterior is again a Beta (conjugacy), with no integral to compute:

posterior = Beta(a + k, b + n − k)
posterior mean = (a + k)/(a + b + n) = w · (a/(a + b)) + (1 − w) · (k/n),  w = (a + b)/(a + b + n)

The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the maximum-likelihood estimate k/n; the prior acts like a + b “pseudo-observations” and its weight w vanishes as n grows.

The likelihood curve is rescaled to area 1 to be comparable; it equals the posterior under a uniform prior.

Advantages, limitations, and the influence on the conclusions

Advantages
  • It answers the question that is asked: the probability of the hypothesis or of the parameter, given the data (a 95% credible interval means “θ is in it with probability 0.95”).
  • It uses prior knowledge (base rates, earlier studies) in a formal way, which is decisive with little data (Example A).
  • Sequential updating is natural: today's posterior is tomorrow's prior, and the answer is available at every sample size, also for n = 0 or k = 0, where the MLE is degenerate.
  • Complex models are built by multiplying likelihoods (Example C) and uncertainty propagates to predictions.
Limitations
  • The prior is a choice. Two analysts with different priors reach different conclusions on small samples (Example B), and “non-informative” priors are not unique (uniform and Jeffreys differ).
  • A badly wrong informative prior needs much data to be overcome (the Beta(30, 70) prior in Example B).
  • Computation: only conjugate models give closed forms; in general the normalising integral needs numerical methods (MCMC, variational inference).
  • The model can be wrong: the independence of Example C is false for real text, so the probabilities are over-confident even when the ranking is useful.

Influence of prior and likelihood. The posterior is a compromise: with few data it stays close to the prior, with many data the likelihood dominates and the posteriors from different priors merge (the Bernstein–von Mises phenomenon, visible in the last table of Example B, where the weight of the prior is (a + b)/(a + b + n)). A sharp prior or a very informative likelihood moves the conclusion more; the prior probability multiplies the odds directly, so a rare event stays rare after a single imperfect test (Example A: 99% sensitivity and 5% false positives, yet only about one alert in six is real when prevalence is 1%). Good practice is to report the prior, run a sensitivity analysis with several priors, and let the data speak when n is large.

Conclusions of Exercise 2

Conditional probability is the language, and Bayes' theorem is the rule that turns P(D | H) into P(H | D): posterior ∝ likelihood × prior. Prior and likelihood have distinct roles (belief before the data, information in the data), and the posterior carries both. Bayesian updating is central because it is the coherent way to accumulate evidence and to include prior knowledge, at the price of having to state a prior, whose weight fades with the amount of data.
